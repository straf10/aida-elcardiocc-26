\begin{abstract}
We present our system for the ElCardioCC 2026 shared task on automatic ICD-10 coding of
Greek cardiology discharge summaries.
The system combines six heterogeneous components --- a fine-tuned Greek BERT model, two
XLM-RoBERTa variants (base and large), a hybrid information retrieval module, a
dictionary-based rule system, and a named entity recognition with entity linking pipeline
--- under a declarative metaheuristic ensemble framework.
Individual models are optimised with task-specific loss functions (Asymmetric Loss, ZLPR)
and per-label threshold tuning on the validation set.
The ensemble fuses component outputs through ten base strategies and four composition
operators, with weights searched via random restarts, hill-climbing, and Variable
Neighbourhood Search.
Our best submission achieves a micro-F1 of \textbf{0.8667} on the official test set,
improving by approximately 1.8 F1 points over the single best standalone model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Clinical coding is the process of assigning standardised diagnostic codes to free-text
medical records. It is a mandatory step in hospital administration, epidemiological
surveillance, and insurance reimbursement. Manual coding is slow, expensive, and prone
to inter-annotator disagreement, making automation an active research priority.

The ElCardioCC 2026 shared task targets Greek cardiology discharge summaries, requiring
systems to predict all applicable codes from the WHO ICD-10 classification for each
document. The setting is particularly challenging for three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low-resource morphologically rich language.} Greek has rich inflection
    and clinical Greek text mixes formal terminology with transliterated English
    abbreviations (e.g.\ \textit{stent}, \textit{TAVI}, \textit{PCI}).
    \item \textbf{Long documents.} Discharge summaries regularly exceed the 512-token
    limit of standard transformer encoders, requiring explicit long-document strategies.
    \item \textbf{Severe label imbalance.} The label space consists of 115 ICD-10 codes
    whose frequency ranges from a single training instance to over 1,000.
\end{enumerate}

Evaluation is performed with a \emph{relaxed} micro-F1 metric that groups semantically
equivalent ICD-10 codes into clinical clusters, so that a prediction of a code in the
same group as the gold label is not fully penalised.

Our system is built around the idea that no single model dominates across all labels and
all document types. We therefore combine a neural-only pathway (Greek BERT and
XLM-RoBERTa) with symbolic pathways (dictionary rules, information retrieval, named
entity recognition) and search for the best label-level fusion strategy using
metaheuristic optimisation on the validation set.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes
the dataset and preprocessing. Sections~\ref{sec:greekbert}--\ref{sec:nerel} describe
the six individual components in detail. Section~\ref{sec:ensemble} presents the
ensemble and metaheuristic optimisation. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports official and
ablation results. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} provides an error analysis, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset Description}

The dataset consists of 2,500 anonymised discharge summaries from Greek cardiology
clinics, provided as JSONL files. Each record contains a patient identifier, the
free-text discharge summary, and one or more ground-truth ICD-10 codes. The data were
split into:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training set:} 2,000 documents
    \item \textbf{Validation set:} 250 documents
    \item \textbf{Test set:} 250 documents
    \item \textbf{Blind test set:} held out by the organisers for official evaluation
\end{itemize}

The total label space contains 115 ICD-10 codes. The distribution is highly skewed: the
five most frequent codes --- Z95 (1,135 instances), I25 (1,167), I21 (865), I48 (830),
and E11 (712) --- account for a disproportionate share of all annotations, while ten
codes appear fewer than five times across the entire dataset (E85, I37, I62, I74, M31,
M35, R31, R57, T83, Z72). Four codes (E85, I37, R31, R57) appear only once.
Table~\ref{tab:label_stats} summarises label frequency bands.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\label{tab:label_stats}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Frequency band} & \textbf{\# codes} & \textbf{\% of total instances} \\
\midrule
$\geq 500$ instances & 5  & $\sim$41\% \\

Documents are moderately long: the mean is 280 tokens, the maximum is 1,218 tokens, and
only 2.4\% of documents exceed the 512-token limit of standard BERT-style encoders.
The average document length is approximately 1,996 characters.

\subsection{Stratified Splitting}

To ensure label frequency distributions are preserved across splits, we apply
multi-label stratified splitting using patient identifiers as the grouping key,
preventing records from the same patient from appearing in different splits. Rare labels
(fewer than two instances) are handled by forced placement to guarantee at least one
training example per class.

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

A common preprocessing pipeline is applied to all splits:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Unicode normalisation (NFD) and accent stripping
    \item Lowercasing (applied only for uncased models such as Greek BERT)
    \item Removal of non-alphanumeric characters outside a safe character set (Latin,
          Greek Unicode blocks, digits, and common punctuation)
    \item Whitespace collapse
\end{enumerate}

For XLM-RoBERTa, which uses a case-sensitive SentencePiece tokeniser, only whitespace
normalisation is applied to preserve case signals present in abbreviations such as
``PCI'', ``TAVI'', and ``CABG''.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}

For the XLM-RoBERTa base track, synonym-based augmentation is applied to mitigate
class imbalance. Terms in the dictionary are swapped with synonyms at a probability
of 0.35. The augmentation multiplier is frequency-adaptive:

Synthetic records are assigned distinct patient identifiers to prevent leakage.

\section{Greek BERT Multi-Label Classifier}
\label{sec:greekbert}

\subsection{Architecture}

We fine-tune \texttt{nlpaueb/bert-base-greek-uncased-v1}, a BERT-base model pre-trained
on a large Greek corpus \cite{greekbert}. The classification head consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean pooling} over all token representations (masked by the attention
          mask), producing a 768-dimensional document embedding
    \item \textbf{Dropout} (rate 0.3)
    \item \textbf{Linear layer} $768 \to 384$ with GELU activation and dropout (0.3)
    \item \textbf{Linear layer} $384 \to 115$ producing per-label logits
\end{enumerate}

The maximum sequence length is set to 256 tokens. The mean-pooling strategy was found
to outperform CLS-token extraction on the validation set by approximately 0.8 F1 points,
because discharge summaries spread diagnostic information across the full document rather
than concentrating it in the first sentence.

\subsection{Loss Function: Asymmetric Loss}

Standard binary cross-entropy treats positive and negative labels symmetrically, which
is problematic under severe label imbalance. We use \textbf{Asymmetric Loss} (ASL)
\cite{ridnik2021asymmetric}, which independently controls the focal down-weighting of
easy negative and positive examples:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASL}} = \begin{cases}
(1-p)^{\gamma^+} \log(p) & \text{if } y=1 \\
(p_m)^{\gamma^-} \log(1-p_m) & \text{if } y=0
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $p_m = \max(p - m, 0)$ applies a probability margin shift to discard very easy
negatives. We use $\gamma^- = 4$, $\gamma^+ = 1$, and a clip value of 0.05. The
asymmetric weighting aggressively down-weights easy negative examples (the dominant
majority) while applying milder focusing on positive examples, resulting in improved
recall on rare labels compared to standard focal loss. The positive weight cap is set
to 8.0 to avoid excessive gradient magnitudes for the rarest labels.

\textbf{Layer-wise learning rate decay (LLRD).} The learning rate is not uniform across
layers. Starting from the classification head, each encoder layer is multiplied by a
factor of 0.9, so the effective LR for encoder layer $l$ (counting from the output) is
$\text{LR} \times 0.9^l$. This ensures that lower layers, which encode general Greek
language structure learned during pre-training, are updated more conservatively than the
upper task-specific layers. On this dataset, LLRD provided a consistent improvement of
roughly 0.4 F1 points on validation compared to uniform learning rate.

\subsection{Threshold Tuning}

After training, per-label decision thresholds are tuned on the validation set in two
passes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global sweep:} a single threshold $t \in [0.45, 0.75]$ (step 0.01)
          is applied to all labels; the value maximising validation micro-F1 is selected
          as the starting point.
    \item \textbf{Per-label sweep:} for each label independently, the threshold is swept
          in the same range. Labels with fewer than 15 positive validation instances are
          not tuned individually (the global threshold is retained) to avoid overfitting
          to insufficient statistics. A label-level gain of at least 0.001 F1 is required
          before accepting a per-label value.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting per-label thresholds range from 0.54 to 0.75, reflecting the model's
varying confidence calibration across labels. Frequent labels (e.g.\ I21) converge to
lower thresholds around 0.54, while rare labels settle near the upper bound of 0.75,
consistent with the model's tendency to under-predict rare codes.

The Greek BERT model achieves a best validation micro-F1 of approximately \textbf{0.8165}
after per-label threshold tuning.

\subsection{Training Variants}

Several variants of the Greek BERT model were trained across development phases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{p2\_long\_asl\_warmlr}: Early variant with longer max length and warm
          LR schedule, used as a baseline.
    \item \textbf{p4\_asl\_mid\_reg}: Mid-regularisation variant with weight decay 0.01.
    \item \textbf{p4\_winner\_lean\_head (final)}: Mean pooling, MLP head $768 \to 384$,
          dropout 0.3, weight decay 0.02. Best overall.
    \item \textbf{mlc\_greek\_bert\_100}: The winner configuration trained for 100
          epochs without early stopping, submitted as a standalone system.
\end{itemize}

\section{XLM-RoBERTa Multi-Label Classifiers}
\label{sec:xlmr}

\subsection{Motivation}

While Greek BERT is purpose-built for Greek, its 768-dimensional hidden size and uncased
tokenisation may limit capacity on long documents with mixed Greek--Latin terminology.
XLM-RoBERTa \cite{conneau2020unsupervised}, trained on 100 languages with a 250K
SentencePiece vocabulary, natively handles code-switching between Greek medical text and
abbreviations such as ``PCI'', ``TAVI'', and ``ACS''. We include both the base (125M
parameters) and large (560M parameters) variants.

\subsection{Long-Document Handling: Sliding Window}

For documents exceeding 512 tokens, we apply a \textbf{sliding window} with stride 256.
Each chunk shares 256 tokens of overlap with its adjacent chunk, ensuring continuity of
context across boundaries. During training, one chunk is selected randomly per document
(encouraging the model to produce reliable predictions from any position). During
inference, all chunks are processed and their logits are fused:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\text{fused}} = \alpha \cdot \bar{y}_{\text{mean}} + (1-\alpha) \cdot y_{\text{max}}
\end{equation}

with $\alpha = 0.5$ (equal mean--max fusion). The mean term captures the overall label
distribution across the document, while the max term ensures that strong local signals
from any chunk (e.g.\ a rare diagnosis mentioned only once) are not diluted by the
averaging. Since only 2.4\% of documents exceed 512 tokens, the sliding window
primarily acts as a safety net rather than a core design requirement.

\subsection{XLM-RoBERTa Large}

The large variant uses the \textbf{ZLPR} loss (Zero-Logit Paired Ranking), a
focal-style ranking loss that encourages larger margin between positive and negative
label scores without requiring calibrated probability estimates. This is particularly
beneficial for multi-label settings with many possible negative classes per document.

\subsection{XLM-RoBERTa Base}

The base variant follows the same sliding-window architecture with a lighter
configuration: batch size 16, standard Asymmetric Loss, and optional K-fold
cross-validation for generating fold-level predictions. It serves as a computationally
cheaper alternative to the large variant and contributes diversity to the ensemble.

\section{Information Retrieval Module}
\label{sec:ir}

\subsection{Motivation and Overview}

Retrieval-based methods approach coding as a \emph{ranking problem}: given a discharge
summary, retrieve the ICD-10 codes whose textual descriptions are most similar to the
query. Unlike supervised classifiers, retrieval requires no labelled training data and
generalises naturally to rare and unseen codes. Its weakness is lower precision on
common codes where distributional patterns are more diagnostic than surface similarity.

The IR module combines three retrieval channels fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).

\subsection{BM25 Retrieval}

BM25 (Okapi BM25) is a probabilistic sparse retrieval model that ranks ICD-10 code
descriptions by term overlap with the query document. A custom tokeniser shared with
the dictionary module normalises both query and code descriptions. BM25 scores are
computed per code and the top candidates are passed to the fusion stage.

\subsection{TF-IDF Retrieval}

A TF-IDF vectoriser (scikit-learn) is built over the ICD-10 code description corpus.
Cosine similarity between the query TF-IDF vector and each code description vector is
computed, and the top candidates are ranked. The TF-IDF channel complements BM25 by
assigning term importance weights globally (across the entire code description corpus)
rather than locally within the query.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval}

A multilingual sentence-transformer model
(\texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}) encodes both the query and code
descriptions into 384-dimensional L2-normalised embeddings. Cosine similarity is used
for ranking. The dense channel captures semantic similarity that is invisible to sparse
models, including synonymous terminology and paraphrases common in clinical language.

\subsection{Reciprocal Rank Fusion}

The three ranked lists are fused using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF):

\begin{equation}
\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{i} \frac{w_i}{k + \text{rank}_i(d)}
\end{equation}

with $k = 60$ (standard RRF constant), equal weights $w_i = 1.0$ for all channels, and
a per-channel candidate pool of size $\max(6 \cdot \text{top\_k},\; 80)$. The final
prediction is obtained by thresholding the RRF score: codes exceeding a minimum
fraction of the top score (\texttt{fraction\_of\_top\_score=0.22}) are selected, up to
a maximum of \texttt{max\_codes=12} predictions per document.

\subsection{Results}

The IR module achieves a validation micro-F1 of approximately 0.45 after tuning. As a
standalone system its precision is limited (0.31) but its recall is high (0.83), making
it a useful diversity contributor in the ensemble for increasing coverage on rare codes.
Table~\ref{tab:ir_ablation} shows the effect of the two key tuning parameters.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{IR module tuning results on the validation set.}
\label{tab:ir_ablation}
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{max\_codes} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{F1} \\

\section{Dictionary Baseline}
\label{sec:dict}

\subsection{Term--Code Mapping}

The dictionary module performs rule-based coding based on a curated term-to-code
mapping stored in \texttt{full\_dictionary.csv} (1,680 unique term-to-code pairs). A
second file, \texttt{full\_dictionary.train\_only.csv}, contains terms derived
exclusively from training annotation patterns, used to prevent label leakage when
evaluating on validation and test sets.

At inference, an Aho--Corasick automaton performs efficient multi-keyword substring
matching over the discharge summary text. Word-boundary checking is enforced to prevent
fragment matches. A blacklist of 35 common false-positive terms is applied.

\subsection{Co-occurrence Rules}

Certain ICD-10 codes are medically related and frequently co-occur. We add a small set
of co-occurrence rules:

\begin{itemize}
    \item If I10 or I11 is predicted, add the other (hypertensive disease hierarchy)
    \item If I22 is predicted, add I21 (re-infarction implies prior infarction)
    \item If I25 is predicted, add Z95 (chronic ischaemic disease often involves implants)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optional Filters}

The module supports optional post-processing filters that are disabled in the primary
configuration but available for ablation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Family history section filter:} suppresses codes matched in sections
          identified as family history, since those codes apply to relatives rather than
          the patient.
    \item \textbf{Negation filter:} removes predictions preceded within a 4-token
          window by negation triggers such as ``without'', ``negative'', ``absent'',
          ``none'' (Greek equivalents).
    \item \textbf{Fuzzy matching:} optional Levenshtein-based matching at similarity
          threshold 85 for handling spelling variation and OCR noise.
\end{itemize}

\section{Named Entity Recognition and Entity Linking}
\label{sec:nerel}

\subsection{Architecture}

The NER+EL pipeline decomposes the coding task into two sub-tasks: (1) identify entity
\emph{mentions} in text (NER), and (2) link each mention to an ICD-10 code (EL). This
two-stage approach allows modular development and provides interpretable intermediate
outputs (explicit mention spans).

\paragraph{NER Component.}
We fine-tune \texttt{nlpaueb/bert-base-greek-uncased-v1} as a sequence tagger using BIO
encoding. A partial CRF layer enforces label consistency constraints across adjacent
tokens. Training uses 6 epochs, batch size 8, learning rate $2.0 \times 10^{-5}$,
weight decay 0.01, and early stopping with patience 2. Dynamic padding is applied to
reduce computation on shorter sequences.

\paragraph{Entity Linker.}
For each recognised mention, candidate ICD-10 codes are retrieved via three sources
ranked and fused:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prior-based scoring:} a frequency counter of (mention text, ICD-10
          code) pairs built from training annotations. This provides a strong and
          computationally cheap prior for common cardiology terms.
    \item \textbf{Dictionary candidates:} the Aho--Corasick matcher from the dictionary
          module expands mentions to candidate codes via direct term matching.
    \item \textbf{Context reranker:} a 200-character window surrounding each mention is
          encoded by \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}. The cosine
          similarity between the context embedding and the embedding of each candidate
          code description is computed and combined with the prior score:
          \begin{equation}
          \text{score}(m, c) = \alpha \cdot \text{prior}(m, c)
                             + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \cos(\mathbf{e}_m,\, \mathbf{e}_c)
          \end{equation}
          with $\alpha = 0.6$ (60\% prior, 40\% semantic similarity).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Document-Level Aggregation}

Entity-level predictions are aggregated to the document level: a code is predicted for
a document if at least one mention was linked to it above a minimum confidence threshold.
An optional document-level boost further promotes codes that are also strong dictionary
matches in the same document, combining the NER-level and document-level evidence.

\subsection{Results}

The NER+EL pipeline achieves a best validation micro-F1 of \textbf{0.7223} at training
epoch 5. It performs comparably to the dictionary baseline but brings complementary
evidence through mention-level context, which is particularly useful for distinguishing
overlapping ICD-10 categories (e.g.\ I21 vs.\ I22 vs.\ I25).

\section{Metaheuristic Ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

\subsection{Motivation}

The six components described in Sections~\ref{sec:greekbert}--\ref{sec:nerel} have
complementary strengths: Greek BERT and XLM-RoBERTa dominate on frequent labels, the
dictionary module covers procedural codes with high precision, IR provides broad recall,
and NER+EL excels at mention-level disambiguation. No single component is uniformly
best, suggesting that ensemble fusion can improve over any individual system.

\subsection{Ensemble Architecture}

The ensemble module receives six binary prediction matrices (one per component, shape
$N_{\text{docs}} \times 115$) and optionally soft scores from the neural components
(which provide calibrated probabilities). It outputs a single fused prediction matrix.
The full strategy space is defined declaratively in YAML, allowing new strategies to
be evaluated without code changes.

The six committee members are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item XLM-RoBERTa Large
    \item Greek BERT (lean head, per-label thresholds)
    \item XLM-RoBERTa Base
    \item Information Retrieval (hybrid RRF)
    \item Dictionary Baseline
    \item NER + Entity Linking
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Base Strategies}

We implement ten base fusion strategies:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Weighted ensemble.} A weighted sum of soft scores (or binary votes)
          is thresholded globally. Weights are optimised on the validation set via
          metaheuristic search.
    \item \textbf{Weighted with majority restarts.} Multiple random initialisations
          of the weighted ensemble are run; the best-performing result across restarts
          is retained.
    \item \textbf{Per-label champion routing.} For each label independently, the
          component with the highest per-class validation F1 is selected. This avoids
          the averaging effect of global weighting for labels where one model is
          strongly dominant.
    \item \textbf{Per-label champion + vote.} The champion prediction is combined with
          a threshold-filtered majority vote from the remaining components.
    \item \textbf{Per-patient score routing.} For each document, the component with
          the highest mean confidence score is used. This adapts to document-level
          characteristics: some documents are more ``retrieval-friendly'' while others
          benefit more from neural classifiers.
    \item \textbf{Gated secondary (IR/NER).} Primary neural models (Greek BERT,
          XLM-RoBERTa large) handle high-confidence documents; IR and NER+EL are
          activated as secondary sources for uncertain cases.
    \item \textbf{Frequency-bucketed thresholding.} Labels are grouped by training
          frequency (rare, medium, frequent); a separate threshold is tuned for each
          bucket.
    \item \textbf{Dual-threshold.} A high-confidence zone (predict), a low-confidence
          zone (abstain), and a middle band (require secondary evidence).
    \item \textbf{Top-K cutoff.} Only the top $K$ scoring labels per document are
          predicted. $K$ is tuned on the validation set.
    \item \textbf{Label correction.} A correction grid search maps frequent
          (predicted, gold) pairs to systematic adjustments, capturing label
          co-occurrence patterns not modelled by any individual component.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Composition Operators}

Individual strategies can be composed using label-set algebra:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Union (\texttt{merge\_or}):} Predict a label if \emph{any} constituent
          strategy predicts it. Maximises recall.
    \item \textbf{Intersection (\texttt{merge\_and}):} Predict a label only if
          \emph{all} constituent strategies agree. Maximises precision.
    \item \textbf{$k$-of-$n$ voting (\texttt{merge\_k2}):} Predict a label if at
          least $k$ out of $n$ strategies predict it. Balances precision and recall.
\end{itemize}

The four composed strategies submitted to the shared task are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{merge\_or\_per\_label\_weighted}:
          per-label routing $\cup$ weighted ensemble
    \item \texttt{merge\_or\_per\_label\_vote\_weighted}:
          per-label champion+vote $\cup$ weighted
    \item \texttt{merge\_and\_weighted\_correction}:
          weighted $\cap$ correction (best overall)
    \item \texttt{merge\_k2\_weighted\_per\_label\_correction}:
          2-of-3 vote among weighted, per-label routing, and correction
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metaheuristic Weight Optimisation}

For the weighted ensemble and its variants, component weights cannot be set by hand due
to the high-dimensional interaction space. We use a two-stage metaheuristic:

\paragraph{Stage 1: Random search with hill-climbing.}
Weights are sampled uniformly from the probability simplex
($\sum_i w_i = 1$, $w_i \geq 0$). Each sampled solution is locally refined by
hill-climbing: a small perturbation is applied to each weight pair (one increases,
one decreases) and accepted if validation micro-F1 improves. The procedure runs for
10,000 total evaluations with 2 random restarts.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Variable Neighbourhood Search (VNS).}
Starting from the hill-climbing solution, VNS iterates over a sequence of neighbourhood
structures of increasing size:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shake:} a random perturbation proportional to the neighbourhood index
          is applied to the current weight vector.
    \item \textbf{Local search:} hill-climbing is applied within the perturbed
          neighbourhood.
    \item \textbf{Accept:} if the local optimum improves the validation micro-F1, move
          to it and reset the neighbourhood index; otherwise increment the index.
\end{enumerate}

VNS is particularly effective here because the validation micro-F1 landscape has many
local optima. The shaking step allows escape from poor local optima without the full
cost of random restarts.

\paragraph{Per-cluster routing (optional extension).}
Documents are clustered by text embedding similarity (K-means with
$K \in \{2, 6, 10, \ldots, 64\}$) and a separate ensemble weight vector is assigned per
cluster. This allows the ensemble to behave differently for post-operative summaries
versus primary diagnostic summaries, for example.

\subsection{Strategy Selection and Submission}

All 14 strategies (10 base + 4 composed) are evaluated on the validation set. A manifest
file records the validation score, component weights, per-label thresholds, and any
errors for each strategy. The five strategies with the highest validation micro-F1 are
selected as official submissions.

Layer-wise learning rate decay consistently improved Greek BERT validation micro-F1 by

recall compared to BCE but less so than ASL.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Rare Label Performance}

The 30 labels with fewer than 10 training instances are the primary source of errors.
For 10 of these (E85, I37, I62, I74, M31, M35, R31, R57, T83, Z72), fewer than 15
positive validation instances are available, making per-label threshold tuning
unreliable. The model tends to either miss these labels entirely (recall $\approx 0$)
or over-predict them, contributing disproportionately to precision losses.

The dictionary module partially compensates: the explicit rule linking the term
``amyloidosis'' to E85 recovers the single training instance of E85 with high precision.
However, for codes with no distinctive lexical surface forms (e.g.\ M35, R57), no
component achieves reliable performance.

\subsection{Dictionary Coverage Gaps}

The dictionary module fails to cover 31 of the 115 label types, including metabolic
(E00), haematological (D47), musculoskeletal (M25, M32, M96), and nephrology codes
(N02, N93). These codes typically require inference from laboratory values not present
in the text, or reasoning over implicit multi-sentence evidence.

\subsection{Label Confusion}

The most frequent error type is confusion between closely related ICD-10 codes that
describe different severity levels or temporal stages of the same condition:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{I21 / I22 / I25:} Acute myocardial infarction, subsequent MI, and
          chronic ischaemic disease may all appear in the same document. Accurate
          assignment requires temporal reasoning (``prior infarction'' vs.\ ``current
          event'') that is not well captured by bag-of-token models.
    \item \textbf{I48 subtypes:} Atrial fibrillation subtypes may be described
          inconsistently in the text, causing both missed and spurious predictions at
          the sub-type level.
    \item \textbf{Z95 over-prediction:} This code (presence of cardiac/vascular
          implants) is heavily over-predicted by the dictionary and IR modules due to
          pervasive mentions of stents and pacemakers. It is the main source of
          ensemble imprecision and motivates the \texttt{merge\_and} correction step.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Confidence Calibration}

The Greek BERT model shows systematic under-confidence on rare labels: predicted
probabilities for true positive rare-label documents often fall below 0.5. Per-label
threshold tuning mitigates this by setting lower decision boundaries, but is unreliable
for labels with fewer than 15 positive validation instances. Future work should explore
temperature scaling or isotonic regression calibration specifically for the tail of the
label distribution.

\section{Training Infrastructure}
\label{sec:infra}

All experiments were conducted using PyTorch 2.0+ and Hugging Face Transformers 4.36+.
FP16 mixed-precision training was enabled for all neural models to reduce memory
consumption and accelerate training on GPU. DataLoader workers were set to 4 with
persistent workers and pinned memory to maximise GPU utilisation during data loading.

\textbf{Experiment tracking.} All training runs, validation curves, hyperparameter
configurations, and model artefacts were logged with \textbf{Weights \& Biases} under
the project \texttt{elcardiocc-2026}. The Greek BERT winner run is identified as
\texttt{greek-bert-p4-winner-lean-head} with tags \{\texttt{mlc}, \texttt{greek-bert},
\texttt{phase4}, \texttt{lean-head}, \texttt{mean-pool}, \texttt{head-384},
\texttt{dropout-03}, \texttt{wd-002}\}.

\textbf{Artefact organisation.} Model checkpoints and best-threshold JSON files are
saved under \texttt{outputs/models/\{model\_name\}/}. Predictions for all splits
(train, validation, test, blind test) are stored under
\texttt{outputs/predictions/\{model\_name\}/} in JSONL format. Ensemble outputs are
organised by strategy slug under
\texttt{outputs/predictions/ensemble\_metaheuristic/\{slug\}/}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We described AIDA-ElCardio, a multi-component system for automatic ICD-10 coding of
Greek cardiology discharge summaries, developed as part of the MSc in Artificial
Intelligence and Data Analytics at the University of Macedonia.

The system combines fine-tuned language models (Greek BERT and XLM-RoBERTa) with
symbolic components (dictionary rules, hybrid information retrieval, and NER-based
entity linking) under a declarative metaheuristic ensemble framework.
Individual components address complementary aspects of the task: neural models capture
distributional patterns for frequent labels, the dictionary achieves high-precision
coverage of procedural codes, and the IR module provides broad recall for rare codes.

The ensemble is optimised via random search with hill-climbing and Variable Neighbourhood
Search over a strategy space of ten base strategies and four composition operators. Our
best submission achieves a micro-F1 of \textbf{0.8667} on the official test set,
representing an improvement of approximately 1.8 F1 points over the single best
standalone Greek BERT model (0.8489).

The error analysis highlights three main limitations: rare-label under-coverage,
dictionary gaps for non-cardiology codes, and label confusion between temporally related
ICD-10 codes. Future work will address these through (i) calibration techniques for the
long tail of the label distribution, (ii) temporal reasoning modules for distinguishing
related codes, (iii) larger language models with native Greek pre-training, and
(iv) cross-lingual transfer from well-annotated English clinical corpora.
